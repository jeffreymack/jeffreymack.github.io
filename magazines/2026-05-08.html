April–May 2026 · MIT Technology Review / Deloitte / PwC
Agent-First Design · Process Redesign · Operating Model

The Mistake Every Enterprise Is Making With AI Agents

They're bolting agents onto broken workflows and wondering why they don't work. MIT Technology Review, Deloitte, and PwC all published variants of the same warning this month: the organizational transformation problem is not technological — it's architectural.

The central finding: leading enterprises don't layer agents onto existing processes. They redesign processes to leverage what agents do well — tireless execution, pattern recognition, parallel processing — and what they don't — judgment, context, trust. Companies that become "agent-first" before redesigning workflows are building digital waste.

The governance question is equally urgent: Who reviews agent performance? Who updates agent workflows? How do local wins get scaled? Organizations answering these questions are building what PwC calls "Owned Intelligence" — institutional know-how that compounds over time and is hard to replicate. Most aren't asking the questions at all.

April–May 2026 · BCG / Fortune / Anthropic / WEF

The White-Collar Automation Wave Is Here — and It's Reshaping, Not Replacing

BCG, Anthropic's economists, and the WEF agree: AI will reshape more jobs than it destroys. But "reshape" is not the same as "safe."

Anthropic's internal economic research — led by its chief economist and shared with Fortune in April — confirms that Claude can already perform a significant portion of tasks in many white-collar roles. The jobs most exposed are not factory workers. They're the educated, $80,000/year knowledge workers: analysts, researchers, writers, coordinators. Precisely the people who currently make AI-purchasing decisions.

BCG's framing is more optimistic but equally clarifying: AI won't eliminate roles wholesale; it will eliminate the low-judgment tasks within those roles, forcing workers toward higher-leverage activities. The WEF estimates 92 million jobs eliminated by 2030, but 170 million new ones created — a net gain of 78 million, concentrated in AI-adjacent functions.

The implication for enterprise transformation leaders: the skill gap is not between humans and AI — it's between workers who understand how to orchestrate AI and those who don't. That's a training and organizational design problem, not a technology problem. And it's where the most defensible human value will live.
